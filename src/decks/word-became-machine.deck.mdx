The Bible in Basic English: S. H. Hooke's translation, New Testament 1941, whole Bible 1949, in Ogden's Basic English --- 850 words, plus 150 for poetry and the specifically biblical vocabulary. A thousand words to say the whole of scripture. The most famous prologue in the canon, and most of this room didn't recognise it on the sheets because every word had been swapped for a plainer one.

That's the whole corpus: John 1, all fifty-one verses, one translation. Nothing else went in.

## between us, we have the whole chapter

![bg right:40%](./assets/word-became-machine/bg-participants.avif)

**1,267** tokens · **263** distinct · **1,266** token pairs dealt across **22**
sheets

no single sheet has the whole picture

```notes
Every one of the 1,266 word pairs in the chapter was dealt to exactly one sheet, and the deal deliberately spreads each context across as many sheets as it will go. So the model wasn't on my laptop and it wasn't on anyone's page --- for the last five minutes it existed only across the whole room. If a sheet had gone home with someone, some contexts would have drawn no hands at all.

That's not a metaphor for how a model is distributed. It's literally where the model was.

And 1,266 parameters is a small model. A frontier model has something like a trillion. Same kind of thing on your lap, a billion times over.
```

## three things Claude\* does that we didn't

![bg right:40%](./assets/word-became-machine/bg-attention.avif)

it looks at **more than one word** of context

it can say what it **never saw** --- `Nathanael` teaches it about `Philip`

it's been taught to **answer**, not just continue

```notes
The asterisk is yours to explain, in one line: Claude here means any frontier model --- ChatGPT, Gemini, Llama. I just need a name to point at.

This room will want this slide, because the obvious objection is "but that's not a real language model". Say each in a sentence and resist explaining any of them.

One word of context is why our sentence wandered --- when I called `the`, nobody could tell whether we'd just written "was the" or "give witness about the". Claude looks back over the whole conversation.

Our model rates any pair it never saw as flatly impossible, however sensible. A real model spreads what it learns across billions of numbers instead of separate cells, so what it learns about one disciple reaches the others.

And ours only ever continues text. Ask it a question and it continues the question. Turning that into something that answers you is another round of training, on conversations.

None of these changed the loop. Look at the context, weigh the options, pick one, write it down. Still just words in, words out.
```

a machine that is **narrative-shaped**

![bg](./assets/word-became-machine/bg-narrative-shaped.avif)

```notes
SAY: what we built this afternoon was a machine, and the only thing in it was John 1.

No Galilee, no Jordan, no body under the fig tree. Just which word followed which. Everything it could ever say was already latent in the shape of the text, and the text was the whole of its world.

The phrase I keep coming back to is from hikikomorphism, on Bluesky, 3 September 2026: LLMs are "text elementals", "entities that are narrative-shaped", and we need a way of talking about them "that acknowledges how weird they are without papering that over with the implication that they're just little guys inside the computer". Their line a few days later: the right stance is to mythologise them rather than anthropomorphise them.

That's the third story. Not a mind, not a parlour trick: a thing made out of narrative, that behaves the way narrative behaves. The room just watched one get built and start talking. The frontier ones are the same thing, made from very nearly everything anyone has ever written down.
```

“the Word became flesh\
and **took a place among us** for a time”

![bg](./assets/word-became-machine/bg-word-flesh.avif)

```notes
John 1:14, in the translation you were holding.

Notice the direction of travel. The incarnation runs from Word toward flesh: toward the particular, the located, the once-only. One body, one town, one span of years. "Took a place among us for a time" --- the BBE's plainness does something the familiar rendering doesn't, because it names what a machine made of text cannot do. It has no place among us. It has no time.
```

{/* _class: hero */}

the word became **machine**

![bg](./assets/word-became-machine/bg-word-machine.avif)

```notes
The language model runs the other way. It starts with flesh --- the millions of people who wrote --- and moves toward text, and then from text toward machine. What comes out the far side is not anyone in particular. It is the average of everything written, with the particularity boiled off.

Small w. This is clearly _not that_ --- and I want to say so plainly, because a room like this one is well placed to hear the difference and the wider culture mostly isn't. It is not a mind and it is not nothing. It's the word become machine: a thing narrative-shaped, made of our testimony about the world, and no more able to be with us than a book is.

Which is not a small thing to have built. It's just a different kind of thing from the one the two loud stories describe.
```

“he himself was not the light:\
he was sent to give **witness** about the light”

![bg](./assets/word-became-machine/bg-witness.avif)

```notes
John 1:8, about the Baptist. Cut this slide first if the ending is running heavy; it's the one most at risk of being too neat.

Text is testimony. Scripture knows this about itself --- John's gospel says outright that it was written "so that you may have faith". A machine made of text is made of witness, and the two stories from the start of the talk are both category errors about a witness: one worships it, the other dismisses it. Neither asks what it's a witness _to_.

You have a vocabulary for that already, and for being careful with it. Most of the people building and selling these systems don't. That's not a reason to keep the vocabulary to yourselves.
```

It doesn't make you an expert: even with a billion dollars in your pocket you can't go out and build your own Claude-level model from scratch. And nothing here tells you whether one should be marking your students' essays.

But it might make you, well --- look at the slide. You can hear a claim about AI and have somewhere to stand: you know what kind of thing it is, and which claims need more than confidence behind them.
