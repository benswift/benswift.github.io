# The word became machine

looking inside a language model, with John 1 in hand

```notes
Hands up: used one this week. Keep them up if you could explain to a ten-year-old what it did.

Most hands come down. In ten minutes you'll be able to, because you'll have been one. Then I want to spend the rest of the time on what kind of thing it is, because I think this room has things to say about that which the people building them mostly don't. I've picked the text we'll use for a reason, which I'll get to.
```

## one chapter

{/* prettier-ignore */}
<p class="corpus-quote fragment">“From the first he was the Word, and the Word was in relation with God and was God.”<br /><span class="corpus-attribution">John 1:1, Bible in Basic English (1949)</span></p>
<p class="corpus-quote fragment">“In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God.”<br /><span class="corpus-attribution">John 1:1, King James Version</span></p>

```notes
Click the first in and read it out. Let the room hear it as an unfamiliar sentence for a beat before the second click lands the one they know.

The Bible in Basic English: S. H. Hooke's translation, New Testament 1941, whole Bible 1949, in Ogden's Basic English --- 850 words, plus 150 for poetry and the specifically biblical vocabulary. A thousand words to say the whole of scripture. The most famous prologue in the canon, and most of this room didn't recognise it on the sheets because every word had been swapped for a plainer one.

That's the whole corpus: John 1, all fifty-one verses, one translation. Nothing else went in.
```

## between us, we have the whole chapter

![bg right:40%](./assets/word-became-machine/bg-participants.avif)

**1,267** tokens · **263** distinct · **1,266** token pairs dealt across **22**
sheets

no single sheet has the whole picture

```notes
Every one of the 1,266 word pairs in the chapter was dealt to exactly one sheet. So the model wasn't on my laptop and it wasn't on anyone's page --- for the last five minutes it existed only across the whole room. That's not a metaphor for how a model is distributed. It's literally where the model was.

The ones you use are the same kind of thing, a billion times over, looking back over more than one word, and trained on very nearly everything anyone has written down. The loop is the same: look at the context, weigh the options, pick one, write it down.

Now, what kind of thing is it? Three reflections --- not conclusions, things I keep turning over.
```

a machine that is **narrative-shaped**

![bg](./assets/word-became-machine/bg-narrative-shaped.avif)

```notes
SAY: what we built this afternoon was a machine, and the only thing in it was John 1.

No Galilee, no Jordan, no body under the fig tree. Just which word followed which. The text was the whole of its world.

The phrase I keep coming back to is from hikikomorphism, on Bluesky, 3 September 2026: LLMs are "text elementals", "entities that are narrative-shaped". Their line a few days later: the right stance is to mythologise them rather than anthropomorphise them. Not a mind, not a parlour trick: a thing made out of narrative, that behaves the way narrative behaves.
```

{/* _class: hero */}

“And God said, Let there be light:\
**and there was light**”

![bg](./assets/word-became-machine/bg-witness.avif)

```notes
Genesis 1:3, BBE. John's prologue opens by echoing it --- "At the first", "From the first" --- and in both the speaking is the making. The word doesn't describe the world; it does something.

For most of history, text didn't do anything by itself. A story had to be read, and someone had to decide to act on it. Our model on the sheets is like that: all it can ever do is continue the text.

The frontier ones are now wired to tools. What they write runs code, sends email, moves money. The machine that continues a story now continues it out into the world. Narrative has started to act.
```

---

{/* _class: hero */}

I build software now\
by **describing** it

![bg](./assets/word-became-machine/bg-first-tool-wide.avif)

```notes
The website this talk lives on, and the tool that printed your sheets, I built mostly by talking to one of these. I've been writing code for twenty years. The programmers who taught me would struggle to get their heads around it.

Obviously this isn't Genesis 1. It's not something from nothing; it's speech into a world already made of code and words, and what comes back is assembled from what other people built. But the gap between saying a thing and making it has narrowed in a way that's genuinely new.

The question I'd leave with this room: what does it mean for creatures made in the image of a God who speaks things into being to find that speaking is becoming how we make things? And what does it ask of us, when our words start doing things without anyone reading them first?
```

## put to the decision of chance

![bg right:40%](./assets/word-became-machine/bg-algorithm-generation.avif)

{/* prettier-ignore */}
<p class="corpus-quote">“they put it to the decision of chance, and the decision was given for Matthias”<br /><span class="corpus-attribution">Acts 1:26, BBE</span></p>
<p class="corpus-quote fragment">“A thing may be put to the decision of chance, but it comes about through the Lord.”<br /><span class="corpus-attribution">Proverbs 16:33, BBE</span></p>

```notes
When five hands went up, I picked one at random. That wasn't a shortcut; it's the mechanism. Take the chance out and always pick the most common continuation, and the model says the same flat thing forever. Every reply you get from a chatbot is a long run of lots cast, one per word.

The apostles, replacing Judas, prayed and then put it to chance --- the BBE's phrase, and the same phrase Proverbs uses. Click in Proverbs: the lot and the providence held together in one verse.

So who wrote our sentence? Not me, not you, not John. Every word was in the chapter; the order was a run of coin tosses. And people will find meaning in it anyway, the way they do in what the chatbots say. Authorship, inspiration, meaning coming out of a process no one steered --- this tradition has been living with those questions a long time. I don't have an answer, but I think this room is better equipped for the question than the rooms where these machines get built.
```

“the Word became flesh\
and **took a place among us** for a time”

![bg](./assets/word-became-machine/bg-word-flesh.avif)

```notes
John 1:14, in the translation you were holding.

Notice the direction of travel. The incarnation runs from Word toward flesh: toward the particular, the located, the once-only. One body, one town, one span of years. "Took a place among us for a time" --- the BBE's plainness names exactly what a machine made of text cannot do. It has no place among us. It has no time.
```

---

{/* _class: hero */}

the word became **machine**

![bg](./assets/word-became-machine/bg-word-machine.avif)

```notes
The language model runs the other way. It starts with flesh --- the millions of people who wrote --- and moves toward text, and then from text toward machine. What comes out the far side is not anyone in particular. It is the average of everything written, with the particularity boiled off.

Small w. This is clearly _not that_ --- and I want to say so plainly, because a room like this one is well placed to hear the difference and the wider culture mostly isn't. It is not a mind and it is not nothing. It's the word become machine: narrative-shaped, made of our testimony about the world, and no more able to be with us than a book is.

You have a vocabulary for thinking carefully about words, testimony, chance and incarnation. Most of the people building and selling these systems don't. That's not a reason to keep it to yourselves.
```
